The research question is what incentives could encourage private companies to stockpile protective equipment and antivirals. This page sets out what 4,121 annual reports from 1,483 companies say about it, and how each category in the explorer was decided.

Figures as of 26 November 2026. Every finding can be reproduced in the explorer with the category named beside it.

Provision is common; stocks are rare

1,057 companies tell the SEC they provide protective equipment to their own people — nearly every large employer in the collection, most arriving after 2020. Almost none writes “we purchased PPE”; they write that they provided it to employees, and the purchase is read from that. The answer to the research question is what happens to that number as you ask more of it:

Companies that…Companies
Mention PPE at all1,307
Say they provide PPE to their own staff1,057
Tie a preparedness activity to that gear47
Describe holding a stock of it for staff8
Act on PPE supply against a future event4
Carry a hand-verified incentive passage9

The rungs are separate questions on purpose, and the gaps between them are the finding. Provision is not a stock: FedEx’s “Providing gloves, masks and other personal protective equipment … to our team members” makes it one of the 1,057 and none of the 8 — across three filings it never describes keeping a supply. A stock is not preparedness, and preparedness is not an incentive. Collapsing any two of these rungs would make the top number the answer to every question, which is how a keyword search gets this wrong.

In the explorer: Provides PPE to its own staff, Ties PPE to emergency preparedness, Holds a stock of PPE for staff, Verified incentive.

Why the demand side is post-2020: the human capital rule

Since 9 November 2020, Regulation S-K Item 101(c)(2)(ii) has required every 10-K to describe the company’s human capital resources; before that it asked only for a headcount. 88% of the companies whose 10-Ks fall under the rule carry the section — 96% of those that are not smaller reporting companies, which may give a headcount only.

It is where the provision of PPE gets said. Of the 1,926 post-rule 10-Ks in which the filer says it provides PPE to its staff, 917 say it inside the human capital section, and 632 nowhere else. The demand side did not appear because employers started buying PPE in 2020; it appeared because the SEC started asking where they would say so.

In the explorer: PPE in the human capital section.

Emergency or the day job?

Each PPE sentence in the human capital section is read with its context — the sentence before it and, for a bulleted measure, the line introducing the list — and tagged. 851 documents (477 companies) tie the PPE to a named emergency; 325 (133) to ordinary on-the-job safety; a document can be both.

The emergency is almost always COVID-19. Natural disasters appear twice — Hanmi’s “response to the pandemic as well as the recent Southern California wildfires”, and Etsy’s disaster-relief giving — and zoonotic disease never. 83% of the emergency mentions were filed in 2021–22.

Only 15 documents from 11 companies show preparedness rather than response: a plan that existed before the pandemic (Kroger “activated our Pandemic Preparedness Plan”, Xerox, Eversource, Pacific Premier, and several business continuity plans) or a standing supply (Tenet and Ardent “maintain availability of personal protective equipment” under infection-prevention protocols).

In the explorer: for an emergency, for everyday job safety, with a plan or supply in place beforehand.

No company describes a stockpile held as preparedness

The stockpile category asks a question the word cannot: whose stock is it? A supplier names a stockpile on every other page and holds none of them — Emergent BioSolutions writes the word 121 times across sixteen filings, and every one is the Strategic National Stockpile, which it sells into under contract.

A government201
Nobody named23
The company’s customers16
The company itself — all ten as goods held to sell10
Other private companies (a forecast)1

The ten self-held stocks are Alpha Pro Tech’s “from time to time, we will stockpile inventory for periods of unusually high demand”, Dermata’s freshwater sponge, Verve’s raw materials and Lakeland’s 2016 stock built “for quick delivery”. The single “other private companies” document is Lakeland 2021: “we believe the private sector will also engage in stockpiling of PPE” — a manufacturer’s market forecast, not a disclosure that anyone holds anything.

So not one document in this category is a company holding PPE or countermeasures as preparedness. That is a finding, not a gap in the rule: employers who do hold such stock describe it as a supply or inventory they maintain, never as a stockpile. None of the ten hand-verified workforce PPE stocks, and none of the 82 workforce-preparedness documents, contains the word at all. Use those categories, not this one, to find companies that hold.

The only direct evidence that private stockpiling happened comes from suppliers: six documents in which Owens & Minor, Lakeland and Alpha Pro Tech report that private customers stockpiled — without naming one.

In the explorer: Stockpile of PPE or medical supplies with Whose stockpile it is; Stockpiling by an unnamed private buyer.

The supply side, asked separately

Two categories look only at the 46 companies that make or sell PPE.

PPE maker holds its own stockpile (7 filings, 2 companies) is the producer keeping finished goods back against a surge, in the first person: Alpha Pro Tech, 2021–26, “From time to time, we will stockpile inventory for periods of unusually high demand”, and Lakeland in 2016, which built “a strategic stockpile for quick delivery” after Ebola emptied its shelves.

Incentive paid to a PPE maker (28 filings, 6 companies) needs an outside payer: a Defense Production Act award (Owens & Minor), U.S. government co-funding of meltblown lines (Lydall), a purchaser investing in a domestic manufacturer (Premier), or grants that pay the maker’s customers to buy — Fire Act, Assistance to Firefighters, Homeland Security and bioterrorism grants (Lakeland, MSA, Versar). A company retooling its own plant is a response, not an incentive, and is not counted.

In the explorer: PPE maker holds its own stockpile, Incentive paid to a PPE maker.

The Strategic National Stockpile is a federal programme, and its filers are its vendors

133 documents from 21 companies name it. By each company’s strongest role: 12 supply it (an award, a delivery, a sale, a maintenance contract), 6 hope to, 3 name it only as context, and none says it received anything from it.

16 of the 21 are pharma or biotech, and the subject is medical countermeasures in all but three documents — Owens & Minor’s Defense Production Act N95 contract (2021, 2022) and Agiliti’s maintenance of the SNS cache of ventilators and powered air-purifying respirators (2022). It is not private stockpiling, but it shows the market a federal stockpile creates for its suppliers — the incentive structure the private side lacks.

In the explorer: Strategic National Stockpile.

Health departments: the SNS’s channel, and a source of orders rather than supplies

580 documents from 248 companies name a state or local health department. Most are licensing and inspection (274) or COVID-era guidance (54). 60 documents from 28 companies show a role in supply:

  • Partners. La-Z-Boy “partnered with local health departments to conduct drive-up COVID-19 testing, distribution of … face coverings”; CMS Energy, CoreCivic, Sanderson Farms.
  • Buyers. Emergent’s NARCAN, OpGen’s tests, AdaptHealth’s ventilators, Exact Sciences’ testing.
  • The SNS’s channel. SIGA, Emergent and Cleveland BioLabs describe the SNS as supplying “federal, state and local public health agencies”.
  • PPE directives. Employers’ PPE measures taken “to comply with directives of state and local health departments” (First Commonwealth and 12 others).

No filer says it obtained PPE from a health department. In these filings the relationship runs one way: health departments tell employers what to do, partner with them on testing and vaccination, and buy from medical suppliers.

In the explorer: Health department and as partner, buyer, SNS channel or PPE directive.

How it was built: what counts as PPE

An explicit mention. A filing counts as mentioning PPE only if it writes out “personal protective equipment” (or “personal protection equipment”) somewhere. A bare PPE acronym does not qualify on its own — in these filings it usually means property, plant & equipment in a tax or balance-sheet table. Once the phrase appears, later acronyms in the same filing are counted.

Three kinds of protective equipment. The phrase is used by three unrelated product families. Only one is in scope: masks, gloves, gowns, respirators and face shields. The other two are body armour — ballistic plates, bullet- and blast-resistant gear, EOD suits, demining aprons — and motorsport impact gear — helmets, neck braces, pads. A phrase match cannot tell them apart, so eight companies spanning 41 documents are excluded from every count: NAPC Defense, American Defense Systems, Safe Pro Group, HighCom Global Security (and BlastGard, the same filer), James Maritime (and Sentinel), Force Protection, National Technical Systems, and Leatt. One of them, Safe Pro, had supplied a passage to the verified incentive set, which is why that figure is 17 rather than 18.

PPE role, and the evidence behind it

PPE role is behavioural: it reads each document for whether the filer describes making and selling protective gear or buying it for its own staff, so it catches makers that sit under a generic industry code. Only the 3,177 documents with an explicit PPE mention get a role. Calling a filing a maker takes two or more supplier cues, not one — a single cue is usually an accident of proximity (UPS shipping PPE out of Asia, a pesticide that requires minimal PPE, a director’s biography naming a former employer). Buying is treated as weaker evidence than selling, so a filing with both reads as both, never as a buyer.

Evidence for the role says how each call was reached, strongest first: the filing states it provides PPE; PPE appears in a list of COVID safety measures; it sits inside an EHS programme alongside fall protection and machine guarding; it appears as a cost line, a supply risk, or a donation.

Two bases are recorded but deliberately not counted as buying. Regulatory only means the filing’s sole PPE sentence describes what the law requires — saying OSHA mandates PPE is not saying you bought any. Staff nearby means PPE and a word like “employees” merely occur close together; hand-checking seven of these found Nasdaq’s “the Staff will inform the Company”, a garment maker’s production notes and a hospital’s PPE rather than the filer’s, so roughly three in seven were real buyers. Both stay findable through the evidence filter.

Supply-chain role

Structural rather than behavioural: it groups industry codes into medical device & diagnostics makers, medical supply distributors, healthcare providers, pharma & biotech, and everyone else — the general employers who buy PPE rather than sell it. There is deliberately no PPE-maker group. There used to be, and it was wrong: no industry code has PPE makers as a majority of its filers, so a bucket built from the plausible codes — ophthalmic goods, plastics, work clothing, sanitation, ordnance, air handling — claimed 119 companies and collected Warby Parker, Tupperware, Sturm Ruger, Clorox, Medtronic and Boston Scientific. The best single code, 3842, is four real makers in fifteen filers. PPE role finds every maker that bucket held and thirty more it missed, Brady and UniFirst among them.

PPE context, workforce preparedness, and the preparedness tag

PPE context separates the two reasons a 10-K mentions protective equipment. Occupational exposure is the day job — OSHA, hazmat, respirators in a mine, bloodborne pathogens in a clinic — a known and continuous hazard. Emergency is pandemic, outbreak, disaster or supply-shock framing. The split is almost perfectly chronological: before 2020 the collection is 237 occupational documents against 7 emergency; from 2020 it is 1,529 emergency against 181 occupational. PPE went from a workplace-safety topic to a preparedness topic in a single year.

Workforce preparedness is a three-way conjunction, all within one window of a gear mention: an emergency or disaster preparedness activity (plan, drill, emergency response team, kit, stock), the gear itself, and the filer’s own workforce. Dropping any one ruins it — without the workforce test a distributor’s disaster-relief sales qualify, without the activity test every “we gave staff masks” qualifies, and without the gear test every business continuity plan qualifies. Passages that merely list the regulations applying to the filer are labelled regulatory rather than counted as its own activity.

The preparedness tag is stricter again, and rare. It needs a future event and an action on supply within a sentence of the mention — not the word “preparedness” in a training agenda, and not narration of what the company did during 2020. Widening the window from 150 to 400 characters moves the count from 1 to 10, so the scarcity is real rather than an artefact. Hand-checking found forward-looking risk disclosure outnumbers forward-looking action.

The stockpile category

The one category with no proximity rule — it reads the subject of the stockpile sentence instead. It began as any mention of stockpile language, which was a poor guide: “stockpile” is the ordinary word for a heap of mined material beside a pit, and extractive filings also abbreviate property, plant and equipment to PPE, so frac sand and lithium producers crowded in twice over. Of the 386 documents that contain the word, 135 are excluded — 83 stockpiles of ore and fuel, 12 of general inventory, and 40 where the word sits in boilerplate with no subject (topsoil, railway ties, petroleum coke, groceries, and the pantry-loading every consumer-goods company described in 2021).

What remains is 251 documents from 54 companies: 222 about drugs, vaccines or medical supplies and 29 about protective equipment. 235 of the 251 are countermeasures or prophylaxis — antivirals, vaccines, antibiotics, the biodefence procurement around smallpox and anthrax. The 16 that are not are ordinary pharmaceutical supply: Aligos, Teva’s European stocking mandates, Dermata’s sponge raw material, Agiliti’s equipment.

Two judgement calls are worth knowing. A drug maker’s stockpile counts as a stockpile of drugs even where the sentence names no product — Teva’s “mandatory stockpiling and high penalties in order to prevent supply disruptions” would otherwise be lost, and it is a regulator requiring a private stock, which is close to the question. That licence is given to pharma filers only; extended to everyone it readmits Kroger and Energizer. And Lakeland’s 2008–12 filings are out, because their stockpile sentence lists a federal chemical-weapons programme it sells into — market sizing, not a stock of protective gear.

The narrower PPE near stockpile language and Antivirals near stockpile language categories apply a 150-character proximity rule instead, and every one of their documents lands on the subject you would expect.

Incentive candidates

“Near incentive language” means an incentive term — grant, government contract, tax credit, capacity expansion, reshoring, purchase commitment, BARDA, stockpile, joint venture — sits within 150 characters of a topic mention, outside forward-looking boilerplate. These are candidates: of 240 PPE candidates read individually, 18 survived review, and one of those, Safe Pro’s body armour, was later excluded with the rest of its product family — leaving the 17 marked verified.

Pharma & biotech, and industries

Pharma & biotech means SIC codes 2833, 2834, 2835, 2836 (drugs, medicinal chemicals, diagnostics, biologicals), 8731 (contract research) and 5122 (drug distribution) — 1,113 documents from 303 companies. The include/exclude choice applies to every number, chart and row, ahead of the categories.

Industry names are EDGAR’s own, from the SEC Division of Corporation Finance’s SIC code list — all 308 codes in the collection resolve to a name. Sector groups them into the ten standard SIC divisions. Three documents from two business development companies carry no code because EDGAR assigns them none.
