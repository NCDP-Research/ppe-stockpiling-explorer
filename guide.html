What this is

A searchable collection of the annual reports U.S. public companies file with the SEC, built to answer one question: what incentives could encourage private companies to stockpile protective equipment (masks, gloves, respirators) and antiviral medicines?

Every document has been read by software that tags what it says: whether the company provides PPE to its staff, mentions a stockpile and whose it is, describes an incentive, names the Strategic National Stockpile or a health department, and more. A smaller set of the most important findings was checked by hand. You can filter on any combination of these tags, read the exact passage behind each one, and open the original filing on sec.gov.

Quick start

  1. Pick a time span and the kind of company under Time and companies in the left-hand panel — for example 2020–26, or Exclude pharma & biotech to focus on ordinary employers.
  2. Tick one or more categories. The number beside each tells you how many documents it would show given your other choices.
  3. Read the results. Click any row to open the passage that put it in your categories, with links to the filing on sec.gov.
  4. Remove a filter with the × on its chip above the charts, or start again with Clear all.

Filters

Search

Finds a company name, CIK number, industry, or any word in the extracted passages — for example Lakeland, 798081, stockpile or their employees.

When the explorer is run locally with its search server, a checkbox appears to search the full text of every filing instead. Quoted phrases, OR, NOT and NEAR(word1 word2, 10) work there. This is not available on the public website.

Categories

Twenty-four tags in five groups, explained one by one below. A few things about how they behave:

  • Any / All beside the heading decides how ticked categories combine. Any shows documents in at least one; All shows only documents in every one — the way to find overlaps, which are usually small.
  • The numbers update as you change other filters. A greyed-out category has nothing left to show.
  • Indented items are narrower cuts of the item above them.
  • Hover over any category for a one-line definition.

What the company does with PPEIn this filing: buys it for staff, makes or sells it, both, mentions it only as a regulatory requirement, or unclear. Or across all the company’s filings: never makes PPE, or makes it in at least one.

Stockpile detail

Both controls apply only to documents in the stockpile category, so choosing either one narrows results to it.

ControlWhat it does
What is stockpiledProtective equipment; drugs, vaccines and medical supplies; or only medical countermeasures and prophylaxis.
Whose stockpile it isA government, the company’s customers, the company itself (as stock to sell, or as preparedness), other private companies, or nobody named. Most stockpiles in these filings belong to governments; companies mentioning them are usually suppliers.

Reading a result

Each row carries tags summarising what was found. Two colours mark the signals closest to the research question:

  • verified — a hand-checked incentive, with its mechanism.
  • workforce PPE stock PPE in human capital section — stockpiling, preparedness and workforce-PPE signals.
  • SNS · supplies it health dept · regulator buys PPE — everything else, in grey.

Click a row to open its passages. Each panel is titled with the category it supports and shows the sentence that put the document there; words you searched for are highlighted. Below them:

  • Open this document on sec.gov — the exact file the passage came from.
  • All files in the filing — the whole submission, which often holds dozens of documents.

A few other tags explain the document itself: exhibit means an attachment to the 10-K rather than its main text; reattributed means the document describes a different company from the one that filed it; body armour — not PPE marks filings where “PPE” means something else.

Questions to try

Which employers say they keep PPE in reserve for their staff?

Tick Holds a stock of PPE for staff. Ten documents, all checked by hand.

Did any company plan for a pandemic before COVID?

Tick with a plan or supply in place beforehand under PPE in the human capital section. Kroger, Xerox, Eversource and others describe pandemic plans they activated in 2020; hospital operators describe standing PPE supplies.

What incentives exist today?

Tick Verified incentive and Incentive paid to a PPE maker with Any. Expand rows to see the mechanism: public funding, purchase commitments, capacity awards.

Is there evidence that private stockpiling actually happened?

Tick Stockpiling by an unnamed private buyer and PPE maker holds its own stockpile. The first is suppliers reporting that customers stockpiled; the second, makers holding stock themselves.

Who supplies the Strategic National Stockpile?

Tick Strategic National Stockpile. Each row’s tag says whether the company supplies it, hopes to, or only mentions it. Set Pharma & biotech to Exclude and six documents remain, among them the only two PPE suppliers: Owens & Minor, under a Defense Production Act contract for N95s, and Agiliti, which maintains the SNS’s ventilators and powered respirators.

Which companies both provide PPE and plan for emergencies?

Tick Provides PPE to its own staff and Ties PPE to emergency preparedness, then switch the categories to All.

What to keep in mind

Most tags are automated. They were tested against hand-read examples, but they can be wrong in both directions. Treat a tag as a pointer to a passage worth reading, and read it before citing it. The categories marked ✓ checked are the exception.
  • Saying is not doing. A 10-K reports what a company chooses to disclose. A company can hold PPE without mentioning it, and a mention is not proof of a stockpile.
  • Annual reports only. The collection is Form 10-K and 10-K/A — not quarterly reports, proxy statements, or anything outside the SEC.
  • Public companies only. Private firms, hospitals run as non-profits, and government bodies do not file 10-Ks.
  • The pandemic dominates. Most PPE language dates from 2020–23. The human capital rule that took effect in November 2020 is a large part of why: it is where employers now describe what they do for staff.
  • Candidates are not findings. “Near … language” categories pair two words that appear close together; many are coincidences.
  • Counts change if the data is rebuilt. The numbers here are from 24 November 2026.

Where the documents come from

Three searches of the SEC’s EDGAR full-text search, limited to annual reports (Form 10-K and 10-K/A):

SearchDocuments
“personal protective equipment”3,216
“antiviral drug” or “antiviral drugs”781
“stockpile” together with “personal protective equipment”302

Documents found by more than one search are counted once in the collection, so the rows add to more than 4,121.

To check the searches missed little, the annual reports of every S&P 500 company were downloaded separately and compared. Of 342 of those documents that use the phrase “personal protective equipment”, 341 had been found by the search — 99.7%. The single miss was added.

Glossary

10-K
The annual report U.S. public companies file with the SEC. A 10-K/A amends one.
EDGAR
The SEC’s public database of company filings.
CIK
Central Index Key — the SEC’s ID number for a company.
Accession number
The ID of one filing; a filing can contain many documents.
SIC code
Standard Industrial Classification — the four-digit industry code the SEC assigns.
PPE
Personal protective equipment: masks, respirators, gloves, gowns, face shields.
Human capital section
The workforce disclosure required by Regulation S-K Item 101 since November 2020: headcount, plus how the company manages and protects its people.
Strategic National Stockpile (SNS)
The federal reserve of medicines and supplies, released to the public through state and local health departments in an emergency.
HHS, ASPR, BARDA
The U.S. Department of Health and Human Services, and two of its offices that buy and fund medical countermeasures.
Defense Production Act (DPA)
Federal law letting the government direct or fund production of critical goods; used for N95 respirators in 2020.
Countermeasure
A drug, vaccine or device for a public-health threat — for example a smallpox antiviral or an anthrax vaccine.
Smaller reporting company
A company below the SEC’s size threshold, allowed to give lighter disclosures — including only a headcount in place of a full human capital section.
